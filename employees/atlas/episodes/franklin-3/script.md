KID: What did it mean?
GUIDE: Back then, there was no United States. There were thirteen British colonies along the coast, and each one did its own thing. They didn't even like each other much. KID: Did they join?
GUIDE: Not yet. Ben went to a meeting in Albany, New York, with a plan to unite the colonies. Everyone said no. But people remembered the snake. Twenty years later, during the Revolution, it showed up again on flags and newspapers.

KID: Silence. Like the virtue.
GUIDE: Exactly. He didn't say a word. But after that day, he was done trying to stay British. He sailed home, and the Revolution was beginning.

KID: Was he part of the Declaration of Independence?
GUIDE: He was. In seventeen seventy-six, Congress picked five men to write it. Thomas Jefferson did most of the writing. Ben and John Adams read it over and made changes. Ben was seventy years old. He signed it, knowing that if America lost the war, the British could hang every man who signed.
KID: That's brave for an old guy.
GUIDE: Brave for anybody. But America had a problem. The British had the strongest navy in the world, and America had almost no navy and no money. They needed a powerful friend. So who did they send?
KID: Ben?
GUIDE: Ben. That same year, at seventy, he crossed the stormy Atlantic in winter to go to France. And the French loved him. He wore a simple fur cap instead of a fancy wig, and they thought he was the wise, plain American scientist who'd tamed lightning. His face was on plates and rings and pictures all over Paris.
KID: He was like a celebrity.
GUIDE: A huge celebrity. And he used it. In seventeen seventy-eight, he got France to sign an alliance with America. French soldiers, French money and French warships. A few years later, at a place called Yorktown, French ships and American and French soldiers trapped the British army, and the war was basically won.
KID: So Ben helped win the war without fighting.
GUIDE: With words, patience and friends. Then in seventeen eighty-three, Ben helped write and sign the Treaty of Paris, where Britain agreed America was free.

KID: Okay. So now there's a country. What's the government part?
GUIDE: The first rules for the new country weren't working. The states were squabbling, there was no real leader, and Congress couldn't even collect money. So in the summer of seventeen eighty-seven, leaders from the states met in Philadelphia to write new rules. A constitution.
KID: Was Ben there?
GUIDE: He was eighty-one years old, the oldest man in the room. He was sick and in pain, so sometimes he was carried to the meetings in a chair. It was a hot summer, and the arguing got bad. Big states against small states. People threatening to walk out.
KID: Like a family on a long road trip.
GUIDE: A lot like that. And one day, when things were at their worst, Ben stood up and asked them to pray. G, listen to what he said, because it's one of my favorite things anyone ever said in that room. He said, the longer I live, the more convincing proofs I see of this truth, that God governs in the affairs of men. And if a sparrow cannot fall to the ground without His notice, is it probable that an empire can rise without His aid?
KID: The sparrow. That's from the Bible, right?
GUIDE: It is. Jesus says it in Matthew, chapter ten. Not one sparrow falls to the ground without your Father knowing. Ben was saying, we can't build this alone. Now, I'll be honest with you. The men didn't vote on his idea. But it's still a famous moment, and it's true that they kept working, and they found a way to agree.
KID: How did they fix big states against small states?
GUIDE: They split the difference. Congress got two parts. In one part, the House, bigger states get more votes. In the other, the Senate, every state gets two votes, big or small. Ben helped push for that kind of compromise, where each side gives a little.
KID: That's smart. Nobody gets everything, but everybody gets something.

GUIDE: Now the ending, and it's a great one. On the last day, September seventeenth, seventeen eighty-seven, the men were lining up to sign. And behind the leader's chair, George Washington's chair, there was a sun painted on it, right at the edge of the horizon.
KID: Was it rising or setting?
GUIDE: That's exactly what Ben said. He told the men near him that all summer, he'd looked at that chair and wondered whether the sun was rising or setting. Then he said, now at last, I have the happiness to know that it is a rising sun, not a setting sun.
KID: Because the country was just starting.
GUIDE: Right. And as Ben walked out, a woman named Elizabeth Powel asked him, well, Doctor, what have we got, a republic or a monarchy? A monarchy is a country with a king. And Ben answered, a republic, if you can keep it.
KID: If you can keep it. What did he mean?
GUIDE: He meant that a country where the people choose the leaders only works if the people keep caring. Voting, telling the truth, being fair, working together. The rules are on paper. Keeping them is up to us. That means you too, G, someday.
KID: G gave a salute.

KID: Was Ben a good guy the whole time?

And one of the very last things he did, in the year he died, was send a petition to Congress asking them to end slavery.
KID: So he was wrong, and then he changed.
GUIDE: He changed. Just like with his little book of virtues. He didn't start out perfect. He kept working on it, his whole life.
