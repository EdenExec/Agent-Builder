1. Why a trout eats a fly: it looks like food (size, then shape, then colour) and moves like food (drag-free drift). The trout decides in a split second.
2. History: Aelian, near the end of the 2nd century, described Macedonian fishermen tying red wool and two rooster feathers on a hook. In 1496 an English book on angling, credited to Dame Juliana Berners, gave fly recipes by season.
3. The box: dry flies float (hatched bugs), nymphs sink (young bugs; about nine of ten trout meals are underwater), streamers are pulled like small fish, crayfish or leeches.
4. Brown trout: golden brown, black and red spots with pale halos. From Europe: German eggs in 1883, first planted in Michigan in 1884. Wary, likes cover, feeds in low light, big ones eat fish. They spawn in fall over clean gravel nests called redds; don't wade on them. Genesis 2:15: work the garden and take care of it.
5. Who else fishes the Boise River: osprey, great blue heron, wintering bald eagles, river otters; about 800 pairs of birds of prey nest in the Snake River canyon south of Boise.
6. In-town Boise River fish (IDFG surveys): rainbow and brown trout, mountain whitefish (native), sculpins, suckers, bass.
7. Bull trout: a char that needs the coldest, cleanest water. Middle and North Fork Boise fish winter in Arrowrock Reservoir and spawn in the headwaters. Threatened; no harvest anywhere in Idaho. "No black, put it back": no black on the dorsal fin, unlike a brook trout.
8. Releasing a fish: wet hands, keep it in the water, gentle grip away from the gills, face it upstream; barbless hooks help.
9. Close: a trout eats a fly that looks like food and moves like food. Next week: the axolotl that regrows its legs, and the dragonfly as the ranch's mosquito hunter.
