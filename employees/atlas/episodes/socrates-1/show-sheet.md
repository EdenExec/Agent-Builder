## The three ideas

- **The barefoot man of Athens.** Born about 469 BC in Athens, son of Sophroniscus, a stoneworker; his mother Phaenarete later became a midwife. Poor by choice, barefoot, one cloak, wife Xanthippe, three sons. Wrote nothing: we know him through Plato and Xenophon, and Aristophanes put him in a basket in the comedy Clouds (423 BC).
- **The riddle from Delphi.** His friend Chaerephon asked the Oracle whether anyone was wiser than Socrates: no one. Socrates, sure he had no wisdom, tested politicians, poets and craftsmen. 'He knows nothing, and thinks that he knows; I neither know nor think that I know' (Apology).
- **The method.** Questions, not verdicts: the Socratic method. A worked 'what is courage?' game (after Plato's Laches). Socrates likened himself to his midwife mother, helping ideas be born. Same questions, different heart: to find the truth (humble) or to trap people (pride in a costume).

## Ten questions for the truck

1. **Where and when did Socrates live?** Athens, Greece, about 469 to 399 BC.
2. **What did his father and mother do?** Stoneworker; midwife.
3. **Why do we know about him if he wrote nothing?** His students Plato and Xenophon wrote about him.
4. **Who asked the Oracle about him?** His friend Chaerephon.
5. **What did the Oracle answer?** No one is wiser than Socrates.
6. **What did Socrates think of that?** He was puzzled, because he knew he wasn't wise, and treated it as a riddle.
7. **What was the difference between him and the politician?** The politician didn't know but thought he knew; Socrates knew he didn't know.
8. **What mistake did the craftsmen make?** Being expert at one thing, they thought they were expert at everything.
9. **What is the Socratic method?** Asking questions so people find out for themselves whether an idea holds up.
10. **When are questions proud instead of humble?** When you ask them to trap people and win, not to find the truth.

## Try this

Play the Socrates game at dinner: someone answers a big question (what is a friend? what is fair?); everyone else may only ask questions back.

## For Kev

- The 'round belly' is Xenophon's (Symposium: Socrates wants to dance to 'reduce to moderate size a paunch'). The barefoot, flat-nosed, bulging-eyed description is in SEP from Plato and Xenophon.
- The courage dialogue is Atlas's simplified game in the manner of Plato's Laches, not a quotation.
- The Oracle story is Plato's (Apology 21a); scholars debate how literally to take it. Told as 'the story Socrates told about himself'.
