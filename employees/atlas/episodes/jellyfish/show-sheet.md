## The three ideas

- **Made of water.** About 95% water. No brain (a nerve net instead), no heart, no bones (a water skeleton). Swims by squeezing its bell; mostly drifts.
- **The sting.** Tentacles carry thousands of stinging cells (nematocysts), each a coiled harpoon that fires on touch and injects venom. They fire even on a dead, beached jelly: never touch one.
- **Top four.** Lion's mane: tentacles about 36.5 m (about 120 ft). Box jellyfish: 24 eyes, some help it steer; very dangerous. Crystal jelly: green fluorescent protein (GFP), now used to light up cells in labs (2008 Nobel Prize in Chemistry). Immortal jellyfish (Turritopsis dohrnii): can turn back into a polyp, seen in the lab. Life cycle: polyp on a rock, a stack of discs, baby jellies pop off. At least 500 million years old as a group, older than trees, sharks and dinosaurs. Not fish ("sea jellies"). A group is a smack; a big swarm is a bloom. Leatherback turtles eat mostly jellies; plastic bags look like jellies to turtles.

## Ten questions for the truck

1. **How much of a jellyfish is water?** About 95%.
2. **What does it have instead of a brain?** A nerve net.
3. **How does it keep its shape with no bones?** Water does it: a water skeleton.
4. **How does it sting?** Harpoon cells that fire when touched.
5. **Can a dead jellyfish on the beach sting?** Yes. Don't touch it.
6. **Which jellyfish has 24 eyes?** The box jellyfish.
7. **How long can lion's mane tentacles get?** About 36 meters, about 120 feet.
8. **What can the immortal jellyfish do?** Turn back into a polyp and grow up again.
9. **Is a jellyfish a fish?** No.
10. **What's a group of jellyfish called?** A smack. A huge swarm is a bloom.
