KID: Alright. Foundation's poured. Now the wood?
GUIDE: Now the wood. This part's a puzzle about weight. You ever been in a bucket brigade? A line of people passing buckets of water hand to hand?
KID: Like old-time firefighters.
GUIDE: Exactly like that. Every pound in a house is a bucket, and it has to be passed hand to hand all the way down to the dirt. Snow on the roof. The roof itself. The furniture. The floor hands it to the foundation. The foundation hands it to the ground. Builders call that the load path.
KID: What if somebody in the line drops the bucket?

KID: Because the window is a hole. The wall can't pass the bucket through a hole, so the beam carries it around to the sides.

KID: So how do they build the frame? Is it just nailing boards?
GUIDE: It's nailing boards, but in an order. Floor first, flat and square. Then each wall gets built lying down on the floor, and a crew stands the whole thing up at once and braces it. KID: I've seen those on trucks. The big triangles.
GUIDE: Those are them. Count them this trip. Now, here's where a lot of people get the story wrong. They think once the frame is up, you put the walls on and you're done. But a house isn't just a box. It's a box with a body inside. Veins for water. Nerves for electricity. Lungs for air.
KID: Lungs?
GUIDE: Your furnace pushes air through metal tubes called ducts into every room and pulls it back. That's the house breathing. So once the roof is on and the house is dry inside, three crews show up, one after another, and run all of that through the open walls. The plumber runs water pipes in and drain pipes out. The electrician runs wires from one big gray box, the panel, to every outlet and light. The heating guy runs the ducts. Builders call that stage the rough-in.
KID: Why that order? Plumber, then electrician?
GUIDE: Because the plumber's pipes are big and stubborn. A drain pipe has to run downhill, always, the whole way, so it gets to pick its path first. Wires are skinny and bendy. KID: And then the walls close up.
GUIDE: Not yet. First an inspector from the city walks through with a flashlight and checks every pipe and wire while he can still see them. KID: So that's why the order matters.
GUIDE: That's why the order matters. The whole house is a line of people, each one waiting for the one before. If the framer is late, the plumber's standing around. KID: Who was the best one ever?
GUIDE: Let me tell you about a man who had to invent the order from scratch. Six hundred years ago in Florence, Italy, they'd built a huge church. Enormous. KID: Why not? They'd built domes before.
GUIDE: Smaller ones. Back then you built a giant wooden mountain inside first, laid the bricks on top of it, and took the wood away when the dome was done. KID: So what did they do?
GUIDE: A goldsmith named Filippo Brunelleschi walked in and said, I can build it with no wooden framework at all. People thought he was crazy. He wouldn't tell them how. But he got the job and did it, fourteen twenty to fourteen thirty-six. Sixteen years.
KID: How, though?
GUIDE: Two tricks. The first is the load path again. He built two shells, one inside the other, with stone ribs between them, so the weight had a path to the walls the whole way up. KID: That's like the bucket brigade but with bricks.
GUIDE: It's the bucket brigade with bricks. GUIDE: Like a window. Or where the chimney comes through the roof, or a deck bolts to the wall. Every hole in the skin is a place water wants to sneak in. Flashing is the bent pieces of metal or sticky tape around every hole that make the water go out instead of in.

GUIDE: Same way a roof does. Think about shingles. Every shingle laps over the one below it. Water runs down, hits a shingle, slides onto the next one down, never gets a chance to go up and under.
