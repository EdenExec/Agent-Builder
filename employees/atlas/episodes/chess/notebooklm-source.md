## What to teach

1. Setup: light square in the bottom right corner. Rooks in the corners, then knights, then bishops. Queen on her own color, king beside her. White moves first.
2. How pieces move: rook straight lines; bishop diagonals and stays on its color; queen both; king one square any direction; knight an L shape (two then one), jumps, lands on the other color; pawn forward one (two on its first move), captures one square diagonally forward, never backward, promotes on the last row.
3. Values: pawn 1, knight 3, bishop 3, rook 5, queen 9. The king has no value because losing him loses the game.
4. Check: get out by moving the king, blocking, or capturing the attacker. Checkmate: no way out. Stalemate: not in check, no legal move, a draw.
5. Castling (once per game, conditions apply) and en passant (exists; learn later).
6. The habit: before every move ask "what does my opponent want?", then checks, captures, threats.
7. Opening rules: a pawn in the middle, knights and bishops out, castle. Don't bring the queen out early. The f7 square is guarded only by the king at the start.
8. Stories: Paul Morphy's Opera Game (Paris, 1858, mate in 17 by developing every piece). Capablanca learning at about four by watching his father (his own account), world champion 1921 to 1927.
9. History: chess began in India as chaturanga around 1,400 to 1,500 years ago, passed through Persia ("shah mat", the king is helpless, became "checkmate"), and the queen got her modern power in Spain in the 1400s.
10. Faith, lightly: the pawn that keeps going becomes a queen ("the last will be first", Matthew 20:16); humility means admitting the move where it went wrong.

## Close

One sentence: You win by trapping the king, and you get there by asking, before every move, what does my opponent want. Next week's question: why would a trout eat a hook made of feathers?
