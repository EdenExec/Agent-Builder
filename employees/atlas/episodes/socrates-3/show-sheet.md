## The three ideas

- **The trial.** 399 BC, aged 70, charged with not honouring the city's gods and corrupting the youth, before a jury of about 500. He would not parade his three sons to win pity. 'The unexamined life is not worth living.' Thirty votes would have freed him; he proposed free meals in the Prytaneum, like an Olympic victor; sentenced to death.
- **Never return a wrong.** About a month in prison (a sacred festival). His friend Crito arranged escape; Socrates refused: never do wrong, not even to repay a wrong (Crito). On his last day he talked with friends, and at sunset drank the hemlock calmly; the prison officer called him the noblest, gentlest and best.
- **Ben's lesson.** At about 16 in Boston, Ben read the Socratic method in a grammar and then Xenophon's Memorable Things of Socrates; 'dropt my abrupt contradiction... and put on the humble inquirer.' He used it at first to win (poor Keimer). Later he kept only modest diffidence: never 'certainly' or 'undoubtedly'; 'I conceive', 'it appears to me'. Socrates: a humble mind. Jesus: a humble heart.

## Ten questions for the truck

1. **How old was Socrates at his trial?** Seventy.
2. **What were the charges?** Not honouring the gods; corrupting the young.
3. **Why wouldn't he bring his sons to court?** It wasn't honest; the jury should decide by what is true.
4. **What is the unexamined life?** A life you never stop to check, like a soul with no checkup.
5. **How close was the vote?** Thirty votes the other way would have freed him.
6. **Why wouldn't he escape?** Never do wrong, even to repay a wrong.
7. **What book did teenage Ben read?** Xenophon's Memorable Things of Socrates.
8. **How did Ben misuse the method at first?** He trapped people to win arguments.
9. **What words did Ben stop using?** Certainly and undoubtedly.
10. **How is Jesus's humility different from Socrates'?** Socrates knew he didn't know; Jesus knew who he was and still served.

## Try this

For one whole day, no 'you're wrong': try 'I think', 'it seems to me', 'what makes you think that?'. Count the catches; report at dinner.

## For Kev

- The death is told gently: last conversation, the cup at sunset, friends present; no description of the poison's effects. Phaedo describes them; that is Kev's call.
- Plato wrote the Apology and Crito; how close they are to Socrates' words is debated. Jury size ('about 500') is the usual reckoning.
- Ben's own account (Autobiography) admits he used the method partly as a religious doubter and to win victories 'neither myself nor my cause always deserved'. The episode keeps the honesty, leaves out the doubting.
