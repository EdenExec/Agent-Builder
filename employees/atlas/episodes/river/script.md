# What you're fishing for: how to read a river
# Atlas (Guide) and Finn (Kid). Episode 2 for the Boise road trip, Thursday 8 October 2026.

GUIDE: Finn, stop the truck in your head for a second. We're on a bridge. Under us there's a river, maybe forty feet wide, moving at a walking pace. Somewhere in that water there's a trout as long as your forearm. You get one cast. Where does it go?
KID: The middle. The deep part. That's where I'd hide if I were a fish.
GUIDE: Everybody says the middle. And the middle is almost always empty.
KID: What? Why?
GUIDE: Because a trout isn't hiding. A trout is working. It's standing in line at a cafeteria that never stops moving, and the line it wants is the one where the food comes to it and the water doesn't shove it around. Today I'm going to teach you to see that line from the highway at seventy miles an hour.
KID: From the truck? You can't see fish from the truck.
GUIDE: You can't see the fish. You can see where the fish has to be. That's the whole trick, and your dad knows it already. By the time we hit Boise, you'll know it too. Three ideas. The fish. The water. The bug.
KID: And then I get to cast?
GUIDE: And then we talk about the cast, and about not falling in. Deal?
KID: Deal.
---
GUIDE: Idea one. The fish. Before you can find a trout, you need to know what a trout wants. And it wants four things, same as you want four things on a long drive.
KID: Snacks, a window, a nap, and nobody poking me.
GUIDE: That's almost exactly it. A trout wants cold water with oxygen in it, that's breathing. Food drifting past, that's snacks. Something to hide under, that's nobody poking it. And as little current as it can manage while it still gets to eat. That's the nap.
KID: Wait, fish need oxygen? I thought they breathe water.
GUIDE: They pull the oxygen that's dissolved in the water through their gills. Cold water holds more oxygen than warm water. Think of a cold soda. Open it, it fizzes a little. Leave it in the sun, open it, it goes everywhere. Warm liquid can't hold its gas.
KID: So a trout in warm water is like a kid in a stuffy car.
GUIDE: Exactly. Around sixty-eight degrees, trout get stressed. Good fly fishers carry a thermometer and stop fishing when it gets that warm. Lee Wulff, one of the great fishermen of the last century, put it this way almost ninety years ago. A good game fish is too valuable to be caught only once. You take care of the fish first. This morning the water coming out of Anderson Ranch Dam on the South Fork of the Boise was about fifty-one degrees. I read that off a government gauge on the river; your dad can pull it up on his phone. Fifty-one is cold enough to make you yelp. The trout think it's perfect.
KID: Okay, so cold water. What about the snacks?
GUIDE: Here's the picture. A river is a conveyor belt. Everything that falls in, bugs, bits of bugs, a grasshopper that jumped wrong, it all rides the current downstream. The trout doesn't go looking for food. It parks itself where the belt runs past its nose, faces upstream, and picks off what it wants.
KID: Faces upstream. Why not sideways?
GUIDE: Two reasons. If it faced downstream the current would push water into its gills the wrong way, and it couldn't hold still. And facing upstream, the food comes to it. It sees the bug coming, tips up, eats, drops back down.
KID: But it's not in the fast water, you said.
GUIDE: Right, and this is the trick. Swimming hard in fast water burns more energy than the bug is worth. So the trout finds a spot where slow water sits right next to fast water. It rests in the slow water with its nose on the edge of the fast water. Food goes by on the fast side. One flick of the tail, grab it, back to the slow side.
KID: It's like standing next to the escalator and grabbing stuff off it.
GUIDE: That's the best way anyone's ever said it. Now the last thing. Cover. Trout get eaten. Ospreys, herons, otters, bigger trout. A fish that sits in the open in bright sun is a fish that gets picked off. So it wants a roof. Deep water is a roof. A bubbly surface is a roof, because the bird can't see through it. A log, a rock, a bank that's been hollowed out underneath. Those are all roofs.
KID: So a perfect trout spot is cold, has the belt going past, has a roof, and is slow.
GUIDE: You just said in one sentence what a lot of men take ten years to learn. Now, three kinds of trout live in Idaho rivers, and they don't all pick the same seat.
KID: There's more than one kind of trout?
GUIDE: Rainbow trout. Native here, a pink stripe down the side, black spots all over, and the tips of the fins on the belly are white. That's the one you'll mostly catch on the Boise. Rainbows like the moving water, the edge of the riffles, the current seams. They're athletes.
KID: What's the second one?
GUIDE: The cutthroat. Idaho's state fish. You'll know it because it's got a red or orange slash under its jaw, like it cut itself shaving. It was here before anyone. It likes the same water as a rainbow but it's easier to fool, which is why there are fewer of them where lots of people fish.
KID: And the third?
GUIDE: The brown trout. Not native. Brought over from Europe more than a hundred years ago. Brown and gold with big black spots and red spots with little blue halos around them. The brown is the sneaky one. It likes the undercut banks, the deep slow pools, the dark edges. It feeds a lot at dusk and after dark. If a rainbow is a sprinter, a brown is a bear in a cave.
KID: Which one's the hardest to catch?
GUIDE: The big brown. It's seen everything, and it sits under the bank where you can't reach it. But here's a fact for October. Browns spawn in the fall. Right now, they're moving and they're aggressive. Rainbows and cutthroat spawn in spring. So on this trip, if your dad finds a dark slow edge, there might be a brown in it feeling bold.
KID: So the answer to where's the fish is different for every kind of fish.
GUIDE: A little different, yeah. But every one of them wants the four things. Cold, belt, roof, slow. Say it.
KID: Cold, belt, roof, slow.
---
GUIDE: Idea two. The water. This is the part you can do from the truck. A river isn't one thing. It repeats a pattern over and over, like a song with the same four parts. Riffle. Run. Pool. Tailout. Then riffle again.
KID: Riffle. Like ripple?
GUIDE: Close. A riffle is where the river gets shallow and fast and bumpy, with rocks sticking up and the surface all broken. It looks like a bad hair day. That's where the river picks up oxygen, and it's where most of the bugs live, under the rocks.
KID: So fish should be there.
GUIDE: Smaller fish, yes, and bigger fish come up to feed there when the bugs are hatching. But it's shallow and it's fast and it's work. Now follow the riffle downstream. It gets a bit deeper and smoother and the water moves steady. That's the run. That's the trout's favorite seat. Deep enough for a roof, fast enough to bring food, slow enough to hold.
KID: And the pool is the deep part?
GUIDE: The pool is where the river slows down and goes deep and dark. Big fish sleep in pools. When it's hot or bright, they sit on the bottom of the pool and wait. But the belt is slow there, so they aren't always eating.
KID: So the middle of the pool was kind of a good guess.
GUIDE: It was a good guess for where a fish hides. It was a bad guess for where a fish eats. And we want eating fish. At the bottom of the pool the water gets shallow again and speeds up, right before the next riffle. That's the tailout. It's smooth and glassy and it funnels everything from the pool into one lane. In the evening, trout line up in a tailout like it's a drive-through.
KID: Riffle, run, pool, tailout.
GUIDE: Riffle, run, pool, tailout. Say it at every bend. Now, inside those four parts there are five little signs. First, the seam. Anywhere fast water runs right next to slow water, there's a line between them. The surface looks different on each side, like two fabrics sewn together. That's the seam. Trout sit on the slow side of the seam with their nose on the line.
KID: Like the escalator thing.
GUIDE: Exactly the escalator thing. Second sign. The eddy. Water hits a rock or a bend and curls back on itself, going upstream in a slow circle. An eddy collects everything. Bugs, leaves, foam. A trout in an eddy sometimes faces downstream because the water there is going backwards.
KID: So the rule is really face into the current, not face upstream.
GUIDE: You just caught a mistake almost every book makes. Fish face into the current. Usually that's upstream. In an eddy it's not. Third sign. The foam line. Wherever two currents meet, bubbles and foam gather into a lane and drift along. Old fishermen say, foam is home. Foam floats on the same line the bugs float on, so if you see a foam line, you're seeing the conveyor belt painted white.
KID: That's cool. You can actually see the belt.
GUIDE: Fourth. The undercut bank. On the outside of a bend the river digs under the grassy edge and leaves a shelf of dirt hanging over dark water. Underneath is a cave. Shade, roof, slow water right against fast water. That's where the brown trout lives. Fifth. Any rock or log that sticks up. In front of it there's a little pillow of slow water. Behind it there's a slow pocket. Fish sit in both.
KID: In front of the rock? I'd think the water would smash them.
GUIDE: Try this at a sink. Put your hand under the tap. The water hits it and splits around it, and right in front of your palm there's a little dead spot. Same thing with a rock the size of a cooler. A trout can sit in front of it, nose on the current, and let the rock take the push.
KID: So depth, speed, and stuff in the water decide where they sit.
GUIDE: That's the rule. Depth gives the roof. Speed decides how much work. Structure, rocks, logs, banks, gives the slow pocket next to the fast belt. When you see a river from the highway, look for where those three line up. Then look for the seam, the foam, the eddy, the bank, the rock.
KID: Does Dad actually look at rivers while he drives?
GUIDE: Every fly fisher who's ever lived looks at rivers while he drives. There's a man named Norman Maclean who wrote a book about growing up fly fishing with his father and brother in Montana, a hundred years ago. The father was a preacher, and he taught his boys that you don't just look at a river. You read it, like a page. The water is telling you where the fish are, and your job is to learn the language.
KID: The river is a page. Okay. I can read.
---
GUIDE: Idea three. The bug. Finn, what does a trout actually eat?
KID: Worms? Little fish?
GUIDE: Sometimes. But mostly, something like nine out of ten meals, a trout eats bugs. And here's the surprise. Most of those bugs it eats underwater, before they ever hatch.
KID: Bugs live underwater?
GUIDE: The ones that matter do. Picture a mayfly. You've seen them, little sailboat wings, two or three tails, they float on top of the water with their wings straight up. That mayfly spent a year underwater as a nymph, a little brown crawler under the rocks in the riffle. Then one afternoon it swims to the surface, splits its skin, pops its wings out, and floats downstream drying off for a few seconds before it flies.
KID: And the trout eats it while it's floating.
GUIDE: That's the moment we live for. The trout comes up, there's a ring on the water, we call it a rise. But remember, before that moment, there were hundreds of nymphs getting knocked loose and drifting along the bottom. Trout eat those all day long, and nobody sees it.
KID: So there's two kinds of flies. The floating ones and the sinking ones.
GUIDE: The dry fly floats and copies the hatched bug. The nymph sinks and copies the crawler. Your dad's fly box has both. When you see rings on the water, you tie on a dry. When you don't, you fish a nymph and let it drift along the bottom. Now, four bug families to know.
KID: Four. Okay.
GUIDE: Mayflies, the sailboats. Caddis, which look like little moths and fold their wings over their back like a tent. Stoneflies, the big ones, some as long as your thumb, that crawl out onto rocks to hatch. And midges. Midges are tiny, like a grain of rice with wings, and they hatch every month of the year, even in snow.
KID: Why would a fish bother with something that tiny?
GUIDE: Because there are thousands of them, and in winter there's nothing else. A big trout will sit in a slow seam and eat midges one after another like you eat popcorn. One isn't worth much. Three hundred is dinner.
KID: So what's hatching now? In October?
GUIDE: Good question, and I checked. On the Boise River and the South Fork in October, there are three things to expect. First, the blue-winged olive. It's a little gray mayfly, about the size of your pinky fingernail. They hatch in the afternoon on cool cloudy days, and the fish love them. The guides call them Baetis. Second, October caddis. A big orange caddis, as long as your thumbnail, that trickles off all fall. Third, midges, all day, every day.
KID: What do you tie on?
GUIDE: Your dad will know, but the fly shops near Boise are saying little olive dries in size eighteen or twenty for the blue-wings, small pheasant tail nymphs, and red or black zebra midges. Fly sizes go backwards, by the way. Size eighteen is tiny. Size eight is big. And on the South Fork there are cream-colored crane flies in the fall that make the fish go crazy.
KID: So the rule is, figure out what's hatching and use the fake one.
GUIDE: That's called matching the hatch. It's the most famous idea in fly fishing. Look at the water. See what's floating. Turn over a rock in the riffle and look at what crawls. Then pick the fly that's closest in size, shape, and color. Size matters most. Then shape. Color's last.
KID: Why size first?
GUIDE: Because the trout sees a silhouette against the sky. A size eighteen bug and a size twelve fly look as different to it as a grape and a plum look to you. Color, it hardly sees from underneath. Think about looking up at a bird against a bright sky. You see a shape, not the colors.
KID: That's a good picture.
GUIDE: Here's how the real masters think about it. Lee Wulff was a famous fisherman in the last century. He designed flies that are still in your dad's box, bushy ones with hair wings that float through rough water. His whole idea was, the fish doesn't need a perfect copy. It needs the right size, floating the right way, in the right place. Get those three, and it eats.
KID: Floating the right way?
GUIDE: That's the cast. Hold that thought.
KID: Holding it.

GUIDE: Okay. The cast. Nobody learns to cast from a podcast. You learn it on the grass with your dad holding your wrist. But I can give you the picture, so when he does that, you know what he's doing.
KID: Fine. What's the picture?
GUIDE: A clock. You're standing sideways to the clock, rod in your hand. Straight up is twelve. The cast goes from about ten o'clock in front of you to about two o'clock behind you. That's it. Ten and two. Not a big swing. A short, firm stroke, and a stop.
KID: Why do you stop?
GUIDE: Because the line does the work, not you. A fly weighs nothing. You can't throw it. What you throw is the line, which is heavy, and the fly rides along at the end. When you move the rod and stop it hard, the rod bends and then springs straight, and it flings the line out. Like a diving board. The stop is what flings. If you never stop, the rod never springs.
KID: So it's like snapping a towel.
GUIDE: Pretty close. And here's the part everyone gets wrong. You have to wait. When the line goes back behind you, it takes a second to straighten out. If you come forward before it's straight, the line's still floppy, the rod can't load, and you get a big ugly pile at your feet. So you stop at two, you wait a beat while the line unrolls behind you, and then you come forward and stop at ten.
KID: How do you know how long to wait?
GUIDE: Norman Maclean's father taught his boys with a metronome. Tick, tick, tick, tick. He said casting is an art done on a four-count rhythm between ten and two o'clock. One, lift the line off the water. Two, throw it up and back. Three, wait, let it straighten. Four, power forward and stop at ten. He said the power comes not from power everywhere, but from knowing where to put it on.
KID: A metronome. For fishing.
GUIDE: It was his wife's, off the cabin piano, and he carried it down to the river. When she worried about it getting wet, he clapped the beat with his hands instead. The point is, it's a rhythm, not a muscle thing. Your dad can clap it for you.
KID: Okay, so the line's out. Then what?
GUIDE: Then comes the part that actually catches fish. The drift. Remember the conveyor belt. A real bug rides the belt at the exact speed of the water. It doesn't skate sideways. It doesn't leave a wake. If your fly does either of those things, a trout looks at it and thinks, that's wrong, and lets it go by.
KID: Why would the fly move wrong?
GUIDE: Because your line is lying across water that's moving at different speeds. The fast current grabs the line and pulls the fly along faster than the water it's sitting in. That's called drag. It's the number one reason a trout refuses a fly. Not the wrong fly. Drag.
KID: So how do you stop it?
GUIDE: You throw some slack in the line, or you flip the line upstream after it lands, a little move called a mend. And you keep the cast short. The less line on the water, the less for the current to grab. A fly riding the current with no drag at all is called a dead drift. Dead drift is the whole game. Ten and two gets the fly out there. The dead drift gets it eaten. A short perfect drift beats a long cast, every single time.
KID: Short and perfect. Got it.
---
GUIDE: Now, wait, but. Here's the thing most people believe about fly fishing, and it's wrong. They think it's about the cast. They've seen the movie, the long line unrolling over the river in the sunset, and they think the man who casts farthest catches the most fish.
KID: It's not?
GUIDE: Nope. Most trout get caught within thirty feet of the fisherman. That's the length of a school bus. The long beautiful cast is the fun part, but it's not the catching part. Here's why. The farther your fly is, the more line is on the water. The more line on the water, the more currents pulling on it. The more pulling, the more drag. So a long cast usually means a worse drift.
KID: So the guy casting far is actually making it harder.
GUIDE: He's making it harder and he's standing in the wrong place. The real skill is walking quietly to the right spot, reading the seam, getting close, and making a short cast with a dead drift. Reading the water beats casting.
KID: So what's the right order?
GUIDE: Find the fish first. Then get the drift right. Then worry about the cast. Most people do it backwards. One more wrong idea while we're here. People think the fish are spooked by the fly line in the air. Mostly they're not. They're spooked by you. Your shadow on the water, your boots crunching gravel. So walk slow, stay low, and keep the sun in front of you so your shadow's behind.
KID: Sneak up on it. Like hunting.
GUIDE: It is hunting. It's just hunting where the animal lives in a window you can see through.
KID: Fish, drift, cast. And sneak.

GUIDE: Safety. This is the part where I sound like your dad, and I'm going to do it anyway, because I want you fishing when you're forty.
KID: Go ahead.
GUIDE: Rule one is the water. In October, it's cold. Fifty degrees. If you go in over your head, your body goes into shock in the first minute and you gasp. That's how people drown, not from being cold for an hour, from gasping in the first thirty seconds. So you don't wade anything that's above your knees and moving fast. Period. Not this year.
KID: What if Dad's with me?
GUIDE: Then he decides, and you listen. When you do wade, you shuffle. Slide your feet along the bottom like you're on ice, one foot planted before the other moves. You cross at the tailout, where it's shallow and wide, never at the run. If you wear waders, you wear the belt cinched tight so they don't fill up. And if you ever do go in, feet downstream, on your back, float to the slow water. Don't fight it.
KID: Feet downstream. Why?
GUIDE: So your feet hit the rocks and not your head. Rule two is the hook. A fly hook is small but it's sharp as a needle and it's flying around your head at the end of a line. So you wear glasses, sunglasses are fine, and a hat with a brim. Every cast. And before anyone casts, you look behind you. Trees, your dad, your own ear.
KID: Have you ever hooked somebody?
GUIDE: Every fly fisher has hooked himself. That's why we pinch the barb, the little backward point that keeps the hook from sliding out. Squeeze it flat with pliers. Barbless. If it goes in your thumb, it comes out. And on the South Fork of the Boise it's the law anyway. One barbless hook per fly, no bait. So you learn it right from the start.
KID: Does barbless mean you lose more fish?
GUIDE: A few. Remember Lee Wulff. Too valuable to be caught only once. So let it go, and let it go gently. Barbless, wet hands, keep the fish in the water, back it goes. That's the deal you make with the river.
KID: Okay. Cold water, knees, shuffle, belt, glasses, look behind, barbless.
GUIDE: You've got it. And one you didn't say. Tell somebody where you are. Always.
KID: Tell somebody. Got it.
