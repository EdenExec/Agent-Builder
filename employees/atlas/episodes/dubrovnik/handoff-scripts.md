# Dubrovnik Old Town Walking Tour: Audio Scripts
For Kim and Dave. Eight short tracks, one per stop. Each is about 2.5 to 3 minutes read aloud at an easy pace.

## Production notes (for the podcast project)
- **Voice:** warm, curious, a little mischievous. A friend who knows the city and is thrilled to be showing it to them. Direct address ("Kim, Dave").
- **Pacing:** [PAUSE] is a two-second beat. [LONG PAUSE] is four seconds, for looking around. Leave real room there.
- **Sound design (optional):** soft cathedral bells under the church tracks, a light Mediterranean guitar bed for the openers. Keep it quiet; they will be outdoors.
- **File names:** `00-Pile-Gate.mp3`, `01-Franciscan-Monastery.mp3`, `02-Sponza-Palace.mp3`, `03-St-Blaise-and-Clock-Tower.mp3`, `04-Rectors-Palace.mp3`, `05-Cathedral.mp3`, `06-Gundulic-Square.mp3`, `07-Jesuit-Stairs.mp3`.

## Order of the walk
Pile Gate, then Franciscan Monastery (just inside, on the left), then down Stradun to Sponza Palace, Clock Tower and St. Blaise on Luža Square, then Rector's Palace, the Cathedral, Gundulić Square, and finally the Jesuit Stairs. The whole loop is a few hundred yards. Stops are one to three minutes apart on foot.

## TRACK 00: PILE GATE ("Welcome to Ragusa")
*Play when they arrive at the gate, before crossing the bridge.*

Stop for a second. Feel that? The stone under your feet is cool, the air smells like sea salt and warm limestone, and in front of you is a wall nearly two kilometers around that has been standing guard for centuries.

Kim, Dave, welcome to Dubrovnik. [PAUSE] Or, as the people who built it would have said, welcome to Ragusa, one of the most stubbornly independent little countries in the history of the Mediterranean.

Here's the story in one breath. For about four hundred and fifty years, from 1358 until Napoleon's army marched in in 1808, this city was its own republic. No king. No emperor. It survived by being clever, by being rich, and by being very, very good at diplomacy. It paid tribute to the Ottomans, it kept Venice at arm's length, and it never stopped smiling while doing it. [PAUSE]

And its motto was a single word: Libertas. Liberty. It's carved over the sea gate, and it's written on a flag that flew over this city. There is a famous Latin line you'll find at the fortress behind you, Fort Lovrijenac, the big one on the cliff to your left. It says, roughly: "Liberty is not sold for all the gold in the world." [PAUSE]

That fortress, by the way, has a trick. The wall facing the sea is twelve feet thick. The wall facing the city is only a couple of feet thick. That was deliberate. If the Republic's own people ever lost control of it, the cannons in the city could blow it apart. Clever little country. [LONG PAUSE]

Now look at the gate itself. Over the arch is a stone statue of Saint Blaise, the city's patron saint. You'll meet him a lot today. This outer gate dates from 1537, and in those days the wooden drawbridge you're about to walk across was lifted up every night. Dubrovnik went to bed with the drawbridge up.

This city was also a pioneer of public health. In 1377 it started holding ships and travelers for thirty days before letting them in, to stop the plague. The word "quarantine" comes from that practice. [PAUSE] So if you're ever feeling sorry for a cruise ship stuck in port, just remember: Dubrovnik invented it.

Okay. Here is your job. Walk across the bridge, through the dark inner gate, and when you come out into the light, stop on the left. You'll see a big round fountain with sixteen carved stone faces spouting water. That's Onofrio's Fountain from 1444. Fill a bottle or splash your face. It's been refreshing travelers for nearly six hundred years.

Then look for the big church front on your left, just a few steps down the main street. That's our first stop. Press play on track one when you're standing in front of it.

## TRACK 01: FRANCISCAN MONASTERY AND CHURCH
*Play when standing on Stradun in front of the church doors.*

You're standing on Stradun, the main street of Dubrovnik, and I want you to look down at it. See how the stones are polished and shiny, almost like marble glass? Seven centuries of feet did that. Yours are now part of the story. [PAUSE]

Behind these doors is a monastery that was already important in the year 1317. That's when the Franciscan brothers set up shop inside the city walls. Their first home was outside the walls, back in 1235, and the city moved them in for safety.

Three things to know. One: the pharmacy. [PAUSE] Right here, the monks opened a pharmacy in 1317, and it is still in business. It's often called the third oldest working pharmacy in the world. Same brotherhood, same spot, seven hundred years. They still make a rose cream here from an old recipe. Ask for it. It makes a great gift, and if anyone asks where you got it, you can say "from monks, since 1317."

Two: the door you're facing. See the stone carving above it? A mother holding her son after the crucifixion. That is a Pietà, carved in 1498. [PAUSE] And here's why it matters. In 1667, a huge earthquake hit Dubrovnik. It leveled almost everything, killed thousands, and brought down the church behind this door. But this carved doorway survived. It's the only piece of the original church you can still see.

Three: the cloister. If you buy a ticket and step inside, you'll find one of the loveliest courtyards in Croatia. Carved around 1360 by a master named Mihoje Brajkov, with something like a hundred and twenty columns in pairs. Look closely at the tops of them. Each capital is carved differently, with leaves, strange faces, little beasts, each one a stonecutter's private joke. [PAUSE] Palm trees and orange trees grow in the middle. It's cool and quiet. It's the best five minutes of peace you'll find in town.

Also, these walls remember the 1991 siege. The tower and cloister were hit by shelling and then lovingly rebuilt. Next time you notice a roof in Dubrovnik that's a brighter orange than its neighbors, that's a roof replaced after 1991. The old city has healed, but it has not forgotten.

Your action: step inside the church, even just for a minute. It's usually open and free, and it's quietly beautiful. Then, if the cloister and museum are open, go in. Whichever you pick, when you step back onto Stradun, turn right and stroll to the far end of the street, about three hundred yards, to the tall bell tower. Take your time. Play track two when you get to the tall building on your left, with the fancy windows, just before the tower.

## TRACK 02: SPONZA PALACE
*Play at the Sponza Palace entrance. Free to enter.*

Walk through the arch into the courtyard. Hear how the noise of the street drops away? Congratulations. You're inside the old customs house of the Republic of Ragusa.

This is Sponza Palace. [PAUSE] It was built between 1516 and 1522, and it is something special: a mix of Gothic windows on the upper floor and Renaissance arches on the ground floor. It was designed by a local architect named Paskoje Miličević, with carving by a family of stonecutters, the Andrijić brothers. They built it like a bank vault dressed up as a palace.

Everything about the Republic's wealth passed through here. Every merchant ship that came into port had its goods weighed, taxed, and stored right in this courtyard. And later the building was also a mint where coins were made, an armory, a bank, and even a school. Imagine a business meeting where silk traders from Venice, spice merchants from Turkey, and Dubrovnik's own sea captains are shouting over each other in half a dozen languages. [PAUSE]

Now look up at the stone arch in the courtyard. There's an inscription in Latin. Here's what it says: "Our weights do not permit cheating. When I measure goods, God measures with me." [PAUSE] That's the Republic's business creed carved in stone. Honest scales, and God is watching.

The best part of the story is this: when the great earthquake of 1667 flattened Dubrovnik, Sponza Palace came through almost untouched. It's one of the few buildings from before the quake you can still see in its original form. So what you're looking at is, truly, the old Ragusa.

The palace is also home to the state archives. They hold documents dating back to the year 1022. Nearly a thousand years of the city's letters, laws, deeds and wills, kept in these walls.

Inside you'll also find a small memorial to the defenders who died in 1991 and 1992. Photographs of faces, lined up on a wall. Take a quiet moment there if you go in. It's a powerful place, and it's why this building matters to Dubrovnik today.

Your action: wander the courtyard, then go through the doorway at the far end into the exhibit rooms, if they are open. When you step back outside, you'll be facing the little square called Luža. Find the tall stone column with the carved knight on top. Walk toward it, and press play on track three.

## TRACK 03: ST. BLAISE CHURCH AND THE CLOCK TOWER
*Play standing in Luža Square, near the carved column, with the church facing you.*

Luža Square. This is where the Republic of Ragusa did its public business. Announcements. Celebrations. Punishments. Feast days. If something happened in Dubrovnik, it happened right here.

See the tall stone column topped by a knight in armor? That's Orlando, known elsewhere as Roland, the hero of the old French epic poems. [PAUSE] He's carved here from 1418 and he stood for the city's freedom. The Republic's flag was flown from a pole beside him. Now look closely at his forearm. The length from his elbow to his wrist was the city's official measure for cloth, called the Dubrovnik ell, about twenty inches. If you were a merchant, this knight's arm was your ruler.

Now turn to the beautiful curved church with the grand front steps. That's the Church of Saint Blaise, in Croatian "Sveti Vlaho." [PAUSE] Blaise was a bishop in Armenia, killed for his faith about the year 316. And his story here is something special.

In the year 971, according to the legend, Blaise appeared in a dream to a priest in Dubrovnik and warned that a Venetian fleet, pretending to visit peacefully, was actually planning to take the city. The warned city was ready. The Venetians left with nothing. And from that day Blaise became the guardian of Dubrovnik. [PAUSE] His face is above the gates. His statue is on the flag poles. His feast day, February 3rd, is still celebrated here with a procession, and it's recognized by UNESCO.

Here's a church detail. This is a baroque church, finished in 1715 by a Venetian architect named Marino Gropelli. The old church burned in a fire in 1706. But one thing was saved, and it's inside: a gilded silver statue of Saint Blaise holding a small model of the city. That model shows Dubrovnik as it looked before the 1667 earthquake. It's the only picture we have of what the old city looked like. Step inside and look for it above the main altar. [PAUSE]

Finally, look up at the tall Clock Tower, nearly one hundred feet high. It has stood in some form since 1444. The bronze bell was cast in 1506. The tower was rebuilt in 1929 after it started to lean. Look for the two little bronze figures on the top who strike the hour. They're called the Green Men, because their bronze has turned green over the centuries. [LONG PAUSE]

Your action: go into the church for a few minutes. Then look for the pale stone palace with the wonderful carved windows on the side of the square, away from the sea. That's our next stop, the Rector's Palace. Press play on track four when you're standing in front of its arched entrance.

## TRACK 04: RECTOR'S PALACE
*Play in front of the palace's arched portico.*

Welcome to the home of the least powerful, most important man in Dubrovnik.

This is the Rector's Palace. [PAUSE] For centuries the city was ruled by a rector who was elected for only one month. One month! And he couldn't be elected again for two years. The idea was that nobody should ever hold power long enough to become a king.

And here's the wild part. During that month, the rector basically lived under house arrest. He lived in this palace, wore a long red robe, and he was only allowed to leave for official duties. He didn't even have the key to the treasury. That was held by other officials. [PAUSE] Imagine being the most important man in the country and not being allowed to go to dinner.

Look at the building in front of you. Gothic windows on the upper floor, a Renaissance arcade down below. It's a layer cake of centuries. The first version was a fortress built in the 1200s. Then, in 1435, gunpowder stored in the building exploded and wrecked it, and the Republic hired an architect from Naples, Onofrio della Cava, the same man who built the fountain at the gate. A famous Florentine, Michelozzo, helped with the redesign. Then in the 1500s it was repaired again after another explosion. The result is a beautiful patchwork.

Now look at the columns of the arcade. See the carved capitals? On one of them is a figure of Asclepius, the Greek god of medicine. [PAUSE] Why? Because that's where the rector sat to hear cases, and the Republic wanted justice to be healing.

Step into the courtyard if the door is open. In the corner there is a marble bust of a merchant named Miho Pracat. He was a wealthy sailor in the 1500s who left his entire fortune to the Republic when he died. And he's the only person who was ever honored with a statue in the Republic's history. Everyone else had to wait. [PAUSE]

And above the door of the great hall inside is one of my favorite Latin lines in all of Europe. "Obliti privatorum, publica curate." It means: "Forget private affairs, care for public ones." Imagine if every leader had that sentence over their office door.

If you'd like a taste of pop culture, this palace played the role of a grand foreign palace in Game of Thrones, in the city of Qarth. But honestly, the real story is better.

Your action: if you want to go in, the Cultural History Museum inside has beautiful period rooms, and the courtyard is a lovely place to sit. Otherwise, walk a few steps along the side of the palace to the grand baroque building with the dome and the tall facade. That's the Cathedral. Press play on track five when you reach its doors.

## TRACK 05: DUBROVNIK CATHEDRAL
*Play at the cathedral entrance on Bunić Square.*

Look up at the dome. Now look at the stone. It's soft, cream-colored, and it looks lit from within, even on a gray day.

This is the Cathedral of the Assumption of the Virgin Mary. And it begins with a legend that is almost too good to be true. [PAUSE]

In the year 1192, the story goes, King Richard the Lionheart of England was sailing home from the Third Crusade when a terrible storm hit near the little island of Lokrum, just off the coast, the green one you can see from the harbor. Richard survived, and vowed that if he made it to shore, he'd build a church in thanks. The people of Ragusa begged him to build it in their city instead, and he agreed, and donated a huge sum to build a cathedral here. Historians argue about whether it's literally true. Most do. But the people of Dubrovnik have told it for eight hundred years, and I like it. [PAUSE]

Whatever the truth, a grand Romanesque cathedral did stand here for centuries. And then came the earthquake of 1667. It destroyed it. The Republic rebuilt it in the Baroque style, hiring an Italian architect from Urbino named Andrea Buffalini. It took decades, and was finished in the early 1700s. What you see is the Baroque rescue of a medieval dream.

Inside, above the main altar, is a painting of the Assumption of the Virgin. It's attributed to the workshop of Titian, the great Venetian master. Not a copy shop trinket, a genuine Renaissance treasure. [PAUSE]

But the real jewel is the treasury, in a side chamber. You'll need a small ticket. Inside are some of the most precious relics in the Adriatic. And the star is Saint Blaise himself, or at least parts of him. His head, in a gold-and-silver reliquary shaped like a Byzantine crown. His hands and a foot in their own jeweled cases. On his feast day, February 3rd, these are carried through the streets in a procession that people have been walking for a thousand years.

There are also more than a hundred other reliquaries, goldsmith work from Dubrovnik's golden age. The city was rich, and it spent that wealth on beauty. [LONG PAUSE]

Here's a fun detail. Under the cathedral, archaeologists found the remains of an earlier basilica from the 7th century. Dubrovnik is a city of layers, and it's still being uncovered.

Your action: step inside and sit in a pew for a minute. This is a good place to rest your legs and cool off. When you're ready, walk out and turn left, around the side of the cathedral, to the bustling market square with a bronze statue of a man in the middle. Press play on track six when you get there.

---

## TRACK 06: GUNDULIĆ SQUARE
*Play in the market square, near the bronze statue.*

You've just walked into the heart of everyday Dubrovnik. Smell that? Citrus and lavender and strong coffee.

Every morning, this little square turns into a market. Local farmers and old ladies in headscarves lay out figs, walnuts, olive oil, honey, dried herbs, and candied orange peel. If it's morning when you arrive, grab a bag of dried figs and some candied orange peel. They're a Dubrovnik tradition, and you'll be glad you did. [PAUSE]

The bronze man in the middle is Ivan Gundulić. He lived from 1589 to 1638 and he's the great poet of the Republic of Ragusa. His masterpiece is a long epic poem called Osman, about the Ottoman world, written in the Croatian language at a time when most of Europe's poets wrote in Latin or Italian. This statue was put up in 1893. Notice the carvings around the pedestal. They show scenes from his poem. [PAUSE]

And Gundulić is celebrated for a particular reason. In his most famous work he praises freedom, again and again. For a republic whose motto was Libertas, he became a kind of national voice. When Croatia introduced its currency, the kuna, it put his picture on the 50 kuna note. He's a literary celebrity.

Around the square: there's the old Baroque pavement, with a design of rays and lines. The market has been here for centuries, since after the 1667 earthquake, when this part of town was rebuilt. The tall building behind us is the back of the cathedral.

And one more name to remember: Marin Držić. A playwright who lived in the 1500s and wrote comedies full of tricksters and wedding schemers. You can see a bit of his spirit here, the haggling and gossip of the market. Dubrovnik loved its comedy as much as its saints. [LONG PAUSE]

Your action: this is a great time for a snack. Pick up something from a stall. Get a coffee in one of the cafes around the edge. Then look up and find the grand staircase at the top of the square. It sweeps up and rises like a theater set. That's our last stop, the Jesuit Stairs. Press play on track seven when you reach the foot of the stairs.

## TRACK 07: THE JESUIT STAIRS AND CHURCH OF ST. IGNATIUS
*Play at the foot of the stairs. Final track.*

Take a look at the sweep of these steps. You've just finished an incredible morning, and this is the grand finale.

These are the Jesuit Stairs, built in 1738. And Kim, Dave, you will recognize the idea. [PAUSE] You were just in Rome. The architect modeled them on the Spanish Steps, the ones you saw earlier on this trip. Just like Rome, they curve in graceful flights and swing up to a church on top. Dubrovnik wanted its own Rome, and it built one.

At the top is the Church of Saint Ignatius, the Jesuit church, finished in the 1720s. It's modeled on the Gesù, the famous Jesuit mother church in Rome. Inside, the apse is covered with frescoes of the life of Saint Ignatius of Loyola, painted by a Spanish Jesuit artist, Gaetano Garcia. If the doors are open, it is a worthy climb. [PAUSE] It's like walking into Rome in miniature.

Behind the church was a Jesuit college. And here's a name you should know: Ruđer Bošković. He was born in Dubrovnik in 1711 and studied right here as a boy. Bošković became one of the great scientists of the 18th century. A physicist, astronomer, mathematician, and a Jesuit priest. His ideas about the forces inside matter helped shape the way later scientists, like Michael Faraday, thought about atoms. The little square at the top of the stairs is named for him. [PAUSE] So when you stand up there, you're standing in front of the boyhood school of a man who helped to write the physics textbook.

If you want one pop culture moment, these steps are famous with TV fans, because the stairs were used for Cersei Lannister's walk of shame in Game of Thrones. Whatever your feelings about that show, you have to admit the stairs make a perfect stage. [LONG PAUSE]

Climb them slowly. Count the steps if you like. They're gentle and wide, and there's a rail along the side. When you reach the top, turn around and look back. You can see the rooftops, the cathedral dome, and the sea beyond.

Kim and Dave, that's the walk. You've seen a pharmacy that's been open since 1317, a customs house that survived an earthquake, a saint who saved a city, a rector who couldn't leave home, a pirate-proof fortress, and a stairway borrowed from Rome. And you did it your way, at your own pace, in one of the most beautiful cities in the world.

Your action: before you head back, find a spot with a view, take a photo together, and send it to the family. When you're ready, walk down the stairs, cross the market square, and follow Stradun back toward Pile Gate. Keep an eye on your all-aboard time. Have a wonderful rest of your cruise. [PAUSE] Enjoy the gelato. You've earned it.
