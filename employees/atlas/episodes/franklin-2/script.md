GUIDE: First, Ben has to make a living. He opens his own print shop in Philadelphia. In seventeen twenty-nine he starts publishing a newspaper, the Pennsylvania Gazette. And a few years later, he prints something that makes him famous.
KID: What?
GUIDE: An almanac. Back then, almost every home had one. It had the calendar, the weather guesses, when the sun comes up, when to plant. Ben wrote his under a made-up name, Poor Richard. Poor Richard's Almanack. It came out every year for twenty-five years, and it sold up to ten thousand copies a year.
KID: He loved fake names. Silence Dogood, now Poor Richard.
GUIDE: He did. And he filled the empty spaces in the almanac with short, funny sayings. You've heard some. Early to bed and early to rise, makes a man healthy, wealthy, and wise.

KID: So he's a printer. When does he do science?
GUIDE: Ben had a rule. If something in Philadelphia was broken, fix it. There were hardly any books in town, so he and his friends started one of the first lending libraries in America. Everyone chipped in, and everyone could borrow.
KID: A library. He did love books.
GUIDE: Houses burned down all the time, so he helped start a fire company, neighbors who promised to come running with leather buckets. Homes were smoky and cold, so he invented a better iron stove that gave more heat with less wood.
KID: Did he get rich off the stove?
GUIDE: The governor offered him a patent, which means only Ben could sell it. Ben said no. He wrote that since we all enjoy great advantages from other people's inventions, we should be glad to serve others with ours, freely.
KID: He just gave it away?
GUIDE: He gave it away. And he did that his whole life. Then, when he was forty-two, he had saved enough money that he could stop running the print shop. And he went all in on the biggest mystery of his day. Electricity.
---
KID: Did people even know about electricity back then?
GUIDE: Only as a party trick. People rubbed glass tubes to make sparks. They stored sparks in a glass jar lined with metal called a Leyden jar, and gave each other little shocks.
KID: Like G and the doorknob.
GUIDE: Just like that. Ben started experimenting. He's the one who started calling electric charge positive and negative. Plus and minus. He even called a row of those jars hooked together a battery.
KID: Battery! Like in my controller?
GUIDE: Same word. He picked it because a row of jars reminded him of a row of cannons, which was called a battery. And then he had a big idea. He looked at the spark from the jar, the crack, the flash, the smell. Then he looked at lightning, the crack, the flash, the smell.
KID: He thought lightning was just a giant spark?
GUIDE: That's what he thought. Lightning was a mystery. Some people even thought it was a sign of anger from heaven. Ben wanted to prove it was electricity, the same stuff as the zap on the doorknob. But how do you catch lightning?
---
KID: The kite.
GUIDE: The kite. June seventeen fifty-two. Ben made a kite out of a silk handkerchief, because silk wouldn't rip in the rain. He tied a sharp wire to the top, to catch the charge. He used a long string made of hemp, and near his hand he tied on a metal key. Then a piece of silk ribbon to hold, so he wasn't holding the wet string.
KID: Who went with him?
GUIDE: His son, William. They walked out to a field when a storm was coming, got the kite up, and Ben stood under a shed, to keep the silk dry.
KID: And then lightning hit the kite?
GUIDE: No, and that's important. If lightning had hit the kite, Ben probably wouldn't have lived to tell it. The storm cloud was full of charge, and some of that charge flowed down the wet string. They waited. Nothing. Then Ben noticed the loose threads on the string were standing up, stiff, pushing away from each other.
KID: Like your hair when you rub a balloon on it.
GUIDE: Exactly like that. So Ben moved his knuckle near the key. And, zap. A spark jumped to his hand.
KID: The doorknob zap! From the sky!
GUIDE: From the sky. Then he touched the key to a Leyden jar and filled it with charge from the cloud. Lightning and the spark in the jar were the same thing. Electricity.
KID: That's so cool. G wants to do it.
GUIDE: G, no. Never. Here's why. The next summer, a scientist in Russia named Georg Richmann tried something like Ben's experiment during a storm, and lightning killed him. Ben was very lucky. Nobody flies a kite in a thunderstorm. Promise me, G.
KID: He promised. Pinky promise.
GUIDE: Good. And by the way, in France, a man named Dalibard had already pulled sparks out of a storm a month before Ben, using a tall iron rod. But he did it by following Ben's written instructions. It was Ben's idea either way.
---
KID: So what good was it? Knowing lightning is electricity?
GUIDE: This is the best part. Lightning used to burn down houses and barns and church steeples all the time. Ben thought, if lightning is electricity, I can give it a safe path. So he invented the lightning rod. A pointed iron rod on top of the roof, with a wire running down the side of the house into the ground.
KID: So the lightning goes down the wire instead of through the house.
GUIDE: Right into the ground. Houses stopped burning. Lightning rods spread across America and Europe, and we still use them today. Look at a tall barn or a church steeple and you might see one. And, you guessed it, he didn't patent it either. He gave it away.
KID: What else did he invent?
GUIDE: Glasses with two parts, so you could see far through the top and read through the bottom. Bifocals. A musical instrument made of spinning glass bowls that you play with wet fingers, called the glass armonica. Mozart and Beethoven both wrote music for it. And on his trips across the ocean, he measured the water temperature and helped map the Gulf Stream, a giant warm river inside the Atlantic Ocean.
KID: A river in the ocean? G, ask Dad about that one on the next fishing trip.
GUIDE: And one more thing for G. Ben loved chess. He was one of the first known chess players in America, and he wrote a little essay called The Morals of Chess. He said chess teaches you to look ahead, to look around the whole board, to be careful, and to never give up when the game looks bad.

GUIDE: Ben would have loved that question.
